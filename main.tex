\begin{abstract}
We measure the detachment size of bubbles on flat surfaces. The results highlight how the maximum bubble volume depends on contact angles and capillary length, and generalize to pendant drops.
\end{abstract}
\maketitle
Pendant drops are physically equivalent to attached bubbles. We shall henceforth refer to the system as a bubble, but the results apply to both.

Pinned bubbles are referred to as mode A by Chesters~\cite{chesters78modes}, whereas spreading is mode B.

Demirkir~\cite{demirkir24life} developed a formula for the bubble detachment size which depends on the advancing and receding contact angles.

Allred et al.~\cite{allred21role} include spreading and receding contact angles in a model of detachment size.

Phan et al.~\cite{phan09surface} model departure size for bubbles with contact angles less than $90^\circ$.

The drop becomes unstable to an asymmetrical perturbation when the contact radius exceeds $3.219\,\lambda$~\cite{michael76equilibrium}.

It is impossible to form stable drops under constant pressure if the radius of the tube exceeds $2.40483\,\lambda$~\cite{pitts74stability}, and under constant volume if the radius exceeds $3.812\,\lambda$~\cite{plateau73statique}.

Tocalculate the bubble shapre we use the method developred by~\citet{fritz35berechnung}. Consider a bubble as depicted in figure~\ref{figProfile}, it is attached to a solid horizontal surface, and $z$ is the height above the substrate. The interface of the bubble satisfies the Young--Laplace equation:
\begin{equation}\label{eqLaplace}
\sigma \left( \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} \right) = p_\mathrm{in} - p_\mathrm{out},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the surface tension, $R_1$ and $R_2$ are the principal radii of curvature at a point on the interface, and $p_\mathrm{in}$, $p_\mathrm{out}$ are the pressures inside and outside the bubble, respectively.

Assume both fluid phases are in hydrostatic equilibrium. Then the pressures at height $z$ are given in terms of reference pressures $q_\mathrm{in}$ and $q_\mathrm{out}$ at the substrate ($z = 0$):
\begin{equation}
p_\mathrm{in}(z) = q_\mathrm{in} - \rho_\mathrm{in} g z, \\
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
p_\mathrm{out}(z) = q_\mathrm{out} - \rho_\mathrm{out} g z,
\end{equation}
where $\rho_\mathrm{in}$ and $\rho_\mathrm{out}$ are the densities of the inner and outer fluids, and $g$ is the gravitational acceleration.

Substituting into equation~\ref{eqLaplace} gives:
\begin{equation}\label{eqHydro_laplace}
\sigma \left( \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} \right) = q_\mathrm{in} - q_\mathrm{out} + (\rho_\mathrm{out} - \rho_\mathrm{in}) g z.
\end{equation}
Let $\lambda \equiv \sqrt\frac{\sigma}{(\rho_\mathrm{out} - \rho_\mathrm{in})g}$ be the capillary lengthscale~\cite{}, and divide through by the surface tension,
\begin{equation}\label{eqPressure}
  \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} = \frac{q_\mathrm{in} - q_\mathrm{out}}{\sigma} + \frac{z}{\lambda^2}.
\end{equation}
To eliminate the pressure difference $q_\mathrm{in} - q_\mathrm{out}$, evaluate equation~\ref{eqPressure} at the apex of the bubble, where $z = h$ and the interface is spherical, so $R_1 = R_2 = R_\mathrm{h}$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{2}{R_\mathrm{h}} = \frac{q_\mathrm{in} - q_\mathrm{out}}{\sigma} + \frac{h}{\lambda^2}.
\end{equation}
Subtracting this from equation~\ref{eqPressure} gives:
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} = \frac{2}{R_\mathrm{h}} + \frac{z - h}{\lambda^2}.
\end{equation}
Multiply by the capillary length:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\lambda}{R_1} + \frac{\lambda}{R_2} = \frac{2\lambda}{R_\mathrm{h}} + \frac{z - h}{\lambda}.
\end{equation}
Each lentgthscale in this equation can be expressed as a ratio of the capillary length, and hence the bubble shapes are self-similar, the results scale universally with the capillary length $\lambda$. This can be understood rouglhy as the vertical scale over which the interface changes curvature due to the effect of buouyancy.

Assuming the bubble is axisymmetric about the vertical axis $x=a$, we describe the interface shape in terms of arc length $s$ from the apex, distance from the axies $x-a$, and the angle $\theta$ between the interface tangent and the horizontal. The two principal curvatures are:
\begin{equation}
R_1 = \frac{ds}{d\theta}, \\
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
R_2 = \frac{x-a}{\sin\theta}.
\end{equation}
Substituting into the pressure--curvature relation yields the governing equation for the bubble shape:
\begin{equation}\label{eqShape}
\lambda\frac{d\theta}{ds} + \frac{\lambda\sin\theta}{x-a} = \frac{2\lambda}{R_\mathrm{h}} + \frac{z - h}{\lambda}.
\end{equation}
This equation governs the curvature of an axisymmetric interface under gravity, balancing surface tension and hydrostatic pressure. We solve the equation using a shooting method, starting from the apex of the bubble at $x=a, z=h,$ with $\theta=90^\circ$ and an initial guess for the radius of curvature $R_h$. We integrate along the path $s$ using the Adams Bashforth step method~\cite{bashforth83attempt}.

For pinned bubbles (mode A~\cite{chesters78modes}) we simply stop when $x-a=r_c$ the radius of the cavity. Figure~\ref{figProfile}a shows the possible profiles in a series of cavities. Every cavity supports the lowest curvature case, indeed $R_h\to\infty$ tends to the trivial case of a flat interface. However, for each cavity radius, there is a limited set of radii $R_h$ which can be pinned, for example, the $r_c=3\lambda$ cavity does not support any bubbles with $R_h<\lambda$. The dependence on $R_h$ can be seen in figure S1 the supplemental material~\cite{supplemental}. For spreading bubbles, (mode B~\cite{chesters78modes}) we stop when $\theta=\theta_c$. Figure~\ref{figProfile}b shows the possible profiles for a series of contac angles. As with the pinned bubbles, each contact angle supports a limited set of radii $R_h$.
