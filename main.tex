\begin{abstract}
We determine the maximum stable volume of bubbles attached to flat surfaces as a function of the contact radius, contact angle, and capillary length. Using a shooting method to solve the Young--Laplace equation, we compute equilibrium bubble profiles and identify the conditions under which each detachment mode occurs: axisymmetric collapse from the cavity centre, lateral instability for large cavities, and spreading followed by detachment from the surrounding surface. The predicted detachment volumes agree well with experimental measurements of electrolytic, boiling, and carbonated bubbles, as well as pendant drops. Our results unify and extend previous analytical estimates and generalise naturally to pendant drops by symmetry.
\end{abstract}
\maketitle
Annually around $10^{11}$kg of hydrogen gas is used in industry, mostly to produce fertilizers via the Haber process, and to reduce metal ores in steel and aluminium making~\cite{kullmann23role}. Currently, more than 95\% of hydrogen is produced from fossil fossil fuels, largely from natural gas via steam methane reforming~\cite{franchi20hydrogen}, which releases significant quantities of CO$_2$. Diversifying away from fossil fuels is essential to maintain a stable Earth system and ensure the long-term survival of human society~\cite{steffen18trajectories}. Water electrolysis driven by renewable electricity is the most promising alternative~\cite{kullmann23role}, splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen without direct CO$_2$ emissions. However, the efficiency of water electrolysis is limited by gas bubbles that form and remain attached to the electrode surface. Attached bubbles block active electrode area, impede ion transport, and increase ohmic resistance~\cite{ross25impact}. It is therefore essential to understand and control the conditions under which bubbles detach, so that electrode geometry and wettability can be optimised to promote early departure.

Two distinct attachment modes are recognised in the literature. Pinned bubbles (mode A~\cite{chesters78modes}) have a fixed contact radius $r_0$ equal to the radius of the nucleation cavity; their contact angle $\phi_0$ changes as the bubble inflates. Spreading bubbles (mode B~\cite{chesters78modes}) have a fixed advancing contact angle $\phi_0$; their contact radius $r_0$ changes as the bubble inflates. The scale over which surface tension $\sigma$ balances fluid density contrast $\rho_\mathrm{out} - \rho_\mathrm{in}$ and gravity $g$ is the capillary length,
\begin{equation}
\lambda \equiv \sqrt\frac{\sigma}{(\rho_\mathrm{out} - \rho_\mathrm{in})g},
\end{equation}
which sets the radius of curvature of the interface due to gravity.

For pinned bubbles, the classical Tate volume~\cite{tate64magnitude} balances buoyancy against surface tension assuming a hemispherical cap with $\phi_0=\pi/2$, giving $V=2\pi\lambda^2 r_0$. The bubble becomes unstable to an asymmetrical perturbation when the contact radius exceeds $3.219\,\lambda$~\cite{michael76equilibrium}, and the bubble is observed to detach from the side of the orifice. It is impossible to form stable drops under constant pressure if the radius of the tube exceeds $2.405\,\lambda$~\cite{pitts74stability}, and under constant volume if the radius exceeds $3.832\,\lambda$~\cite{plateau73statique}. For orifices larger than this, the liquid is observed to drip from the side of the orifice instead of forming a stable drop~\cite{sasetty23stability}.

The study of spreading bubble departure goes back to Fritz~\cite{fritz35berechnung}, who numerivally measured the maximum volume $V$ of bubbles depending on the contact angle $\phi_0$, obtaining an expression for the detacthment volume of spreading bubbles $V=27.5\lambda^3\phi_0^3$.
Phan et al.~\cite{phan09surface} model departure size for bubbles with contact angles less than $\pi/2$.
Recent models for the detachment size take contact angle hysteresis into account and use the spreading and receding contact angles as input~\cite{allred21role,demirkir24life}.

We aim to unify this research by considering pinned and spreading behaviour together. We ask two fundamental questions: 1. \emph{How large does a bubble grow before it detaches?} And 2. \emph{How does it detach?} We answer these questions by considering a simplified system of a circular cavity containing an already nucleated bubble, it is surrounded by a smooth surface without contact angle hysteresis, and a quiescent fluid. This system is physically equivalent to a pendant drop. In this letter, we shall refer to the system as a bubble, but our results apply to both. We measure the detachment size for pinned and spreading bubbles for the entire range of cavity sizes, capillary lengths, and contact angles.

Tocalculate the bubble shapre we use the method developred by~\citet{fritz35berechnung}. Consider a bubble as depicted in figure~\ref{figProfile}, it is attached to a solid horizontal surface, and $z$ is the height above the substrate. The interface of the bubble satisfies the Young--Laplace equation:
\begin{equation}\label{eqLaplace}
\sigma \left( \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} \right) = p_\mathrm{in} - p_\mathrm{out},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the surface tension, $R_1$ and $R_2$ are the principal radii of curvature at a point on the interface, and $p_\mathrm{in}$, $p_\mathrm{out}$ are the pressures inside and outside the bubble, respectively.

Assume both fluid phases are in hydrostatic equilibrium. Then the pressures at height $z$ are given in terms of reference pressures $q_\mathrm{in}$ and $q_\mathrm{out}$ at the substrate ($z = 0$):
\begin{equation}
p_\mathrm{in}(z) = q_\mathrm{in} - \rho_\mathrm{in} g z, \\
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
p_\mathrm{out}(z) = q_\mathrm{out} - \rho_\mathrm{out} g z,
\end{equation}
Substituting into equation~\ref{eqLaplace} gives:
\begin{equation}\label{eqHydro_laplace}
\sigma \left( \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} \right) = q_\mathrm{in} - q_\mathrm{out} + (\rho_\mathrm{out} - \rho_\mathrm{in}) g z.
\end{equation}
 Divide through by the surface tension,
\begin{equation}\label{eqPressure}
  \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} = \frac{q_\mathrm{in} - q_\mathrm{out}}{\sigma} + \frac{z}{\lambda^2}.
\end{equation}
To eliminate the pressure difference $q_\mathrm{in} - q_\mathrm{out}$, evaluate equation~\ref{eqPressure} at the apex of the bubble, where $z = h$ and the interface is spherical, so $R_1 = R_2 = R_\mathrm{h}$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{2}{R_\mathrm{h}} = \frac{q_\mathrm{in} - q_\mathrm{out}}{\sigma} + \frac{h}{\lambda^2}.
\end{equation}
Subtracting this from equation~\ref{eqPressure} gives:
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} = \frac{2}{R_\mathrm{h}} + \frac{z - h}{\lambda^2}.
\end{equation}
Multiply by the capillary length:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\lambda}{R_1} + \frac{\lambda}{R_2} = \frac{2\lambda}{R_\mathrm{h}} + \frac{z - h}{\lambda}.
\end{equation}
Each lentgthscale in this equation can be expressed as a ratio of the capillary length, and hence the bubble shapes are self-similar, the results scale universally with the capillary length $\lambda$.

Assuming the bubble is axisymmetric about the vertical axis $x=a$, we describe the interface shape in terms of arc length $s$ from the apex, distance from the axies $x-a$, and the angle $\phi$ between the interface tangent and the horizontal. The two principal curvatures are:
\begin{equation}
R_1 = \frac{ds}{d\phi}, \\
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
R_2 = \frac{r}{\sin\phi}.
\end{equation}
Substituting into the pressure--curvature relation yields the governing equation for the bubble shape:
\begin{equation}\label{eqShape}
\lambda\frac{d\phi}{ds} + \frac{\lambda\sin\phi}{r} = \frac{2\lambda}{R_\mathrm{h}} + \frac{z - h}{\lambda}.
\end{equation}
This equation governs the curvature of an axisymmetric interface under gravity, balancing surface tension and hydrostatic pressure. We solve the equation using a shooting method, starting from the apex of the bubble at $r=0, z=h,$ with $\phi=\pi$ and an initial guess for the radius of curvature $R_h$. We integrate along the path $s$ using the Adams Bashforth step method~\cite{bashforth83attempt}, which, incidentally, was developed to solve this very equation.

\begin{equation}
V=\int_0^h\pi r^2dz
\end{equation}
We measure the bubble volume above the contact line, however, some gas may remain attached to the surface after bubble breakage, so the true detached volume may be less. We assume that the bubble nucleates in a cavity of radius $r_0$ with zero volume and zero height, at the bottom left of figure~\ref{figHeight}. The height $h$ increases as the bubble grows. The bubble remains pinned, and lies on the $r_0$ curve until one of three conditions is met;
\begin{enumerate}
\item If the contact angle $\phi_0$ becomes less than the receeding contact angle on the surface surrounding the cavity, the contact line will become unpinned and spread across the surface, as described in~\cite{chesters78modes}. The bubble height and volume now follows a curve in figure~\ref{figHeight}b.
\item If the contact angle $\phi_0$ becomes equal to $\pi$, the interface becomes unstable to non-axissymmetric perturbations~\cite{michael76equilibrium}, the peak slides to one side of the cavity, and the bubble detaches from the side of the cavity. This occurs for $r_0>3.219\lambda$. **and the limiting case can be seen in figure~\ref{figHeight} a.**
\item If the volume of the bubble reaches the maximum possible volume for the $r_0$ curve, its height can increase without an increase in volume, and it detaches. This replresented by the end of each $r_0$ curve in figure~\ref{figHeight}a. 
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{figPinVol} shows the detachment volume $V$ of pinned bubbles with cavity radius $r_0$. We see that the detachment volume initially increases with cavity radius, and it follows a trend close to that of the Tate volume~\cite{tate64magnitude}. However, there are deviation due to the nonspherical shape of the interface under buoyancy effects. The deviations from the Tate volume are supported by experimental measurements of bubbles emitted from tubes in water~\cite{lesage13experimental}, and bubbles in carbonated water emitted from millimetric cavities~\cite{mori01bubble}, and pendant drops~\cite{gunde01measurement}. For cavities with $r_0>3.219$, the contact angle reaches $\phi_0=\pi$ and the interface becomes unstable to non-axissymmetric perturbations~\cite{michael76equilibrium}. The bubble slides to one side of the cavity, and detaches from the side of the cavity. This occurs for $r_0>3.219\lambda$. The maximum possible cavity which can support a bubble has radius $r_0=3.832\lambda$~\cite{plateau73statique}, above this radius the bubble will immediately detatch from the side of the cavity.

We have reported on the stability of drops in constant volume conditions.

We now return to the three questions posed at the outset.
 
\textit{Answer to Q1.}
The detachment volume depends on whether the bubble is pinned or spreading.
For pinned bubbles, the detachment volume rises nearly linearly with cavity radius, closely following the Tate prediction $V_\mathrm{Tate} = 2\pi\lambda^2 r_0$, with quantitative corrections arising from the non-spherical interface shape under buoyancy (figure~\ref{figPinVol}).
For spreading bubbles, the detachment volume increases monotonically with contact angle and saturates at $V_s = 18.96\,\lambda^3$ as $\phi_0 \to \pi$, below the Fritz extrapolation (figure~\ref{figSpreVol}).
Both results are confirmed by independent experimental datasets spanning electrolytic, boiling, and carbonated bubbles, as well as pendant drops.
 
\textit{Answer to Q2.}
The detachment mode is determined by which threshold is reached first during bubble growth (figure~\ref{figTree}).
A pinned bubble detaches axisymmetrically from the cavity centre when its volume reaches the maximum of the $r_0$ curve; it detaches laterally when the contact angle reaches $\phi_0 = \pi$, which occurs only for $r_0 > 3.219\,\lambda$; and it transitions to the spreading mode when the contact angle falls below the receding contact angle of the surrounding surface.
A spreading bubble then detaches from the surface when its volume reaches the maximum of the $\phi_0$ curve.
No stable axisymmetric bubble exists for $r_0 > 3.832\,\lambda$, above which immediate lateral detachment occurs.
