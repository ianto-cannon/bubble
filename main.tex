\begin{abstract}
We determine the maximum stable volume of bubbles attached to flat surfaces as a function of the contact radius, contact angle, and capillary length. Using a shooting method to solve the Young--Laplace equation, we compute equilibrium bubble profiles and identify the conditions under which three detachment modes occur: axisymmetric necking from the cavity centre, lateral instability for large cavities, and spreading followed by axisymmetric necking from the surrounding surface. The predicted detachment volumes agree well with experimental measurements of electrolytic, boiling, and carbonated bubbles, as well as pendant drops. Our results unify and extend previous analytical estimates and apply directly to pendant drops by symmetry.
\end{abstract}
\maketitle

Annually, around $10^{11}$\,kg of hydrogen gas is used in industry, mostly to produce fertilisers via the Haber process and to reduce metal ores in steelmaking and aluminium production~\cite{kullmann23role}. Currently, more than 95\% of hydrogen is produced from fossil fuels, largely from natural gas via steam methane reforming~\cite{franchi20hydrogen}, which releases significant quantities of CO$_2$. Diversifying away from fossil fuels is essential to maintain a stable Earth climate system and ensure the long-term survival of human society~\cite{steffen18trajectories}. Water electrolysis driven by renewable electricity is the most promising alternative~\cite{kullmann23role}, splitting water into hydrogen and oxygen without direct CO$_2$ emissions. However, the efficiency of water electrolysis is limited by gas bubbles that form and remain attached to the electrode surface. Attached bubbles block active electrode area, impede ion transport, and increase ohmic resistance~\cite{vogt89problem,angulo20influence,ross25impact}. It is therefore essential to understand and control the conditions under which bubbles detach, so that electrode geometry and wettability can be optimised to promote early departure.

The scale over which surface tension $\sigma$ balances fluid density contrast $\rho_\mathrm{out} - \rho_\mathrm{in}$ and gravity $g$ is the capillary length~\cite{laplace98traite},
\begin{equation}
\lambda \equiv \sqrt\frac{\sigma}{(\rho_\mathrm{out} - \rho_\mathrm{in})g}.
\end{equation}
This sets the characteristic radius of curvature of the bubble interface due to gravity. Two distinct attachment modes are recognised in the literature. Pinned bubbles (mode~A~\cite{chesters78modes}) have a fixed foot radius $r_0$ equal to the radius $r_c$ of the nucleation cavity; their foot angle $\phi_0$ changes as the bubble inflates. Spreading bubbles (mode~B~\cite{chesters78modes}) have foot angle $\phi_0$ fixed at the contact angle $\phi_c$ of the system; their foot radius $r_0$ changes as the bubble inflates.

For pinned bubbles, the classical Tate volume~\cite{tate64magnitude} assumes a the bubble detaches when it is a hemispherical cap with $\phi_0=\pi/2$ and balances the buoyancy force against the surface tension force, giving a linear expression for the detachment size $V=2\pi\lambda^2 r_c$. Chesters~\cite{chesters77analytical} refined this model in the limit $r_c/\lambda\to0$ and calculated detachment volumes, also by assuming the bubble detaches when $\phi_0=\pi/2$. Gerlach et al.~\cite{gerlach05quasistatic} compared measurements of bubble departure from Teflon cavities with radii in the range $0.2\lambda<r_c<0.7\lambda$ with simulations. Stability analyses have shown that the bubble becomes unstable to an asymmetrical perturbation when the cavity radius exceeds $3.219\,\lambda$~\cite{michael76equilibrium}, and that no stable bubble exists at fixed volume when the cavity radius exceeds $3.832\,\lambda$~\cite{plateau73statique}. For cavities larger than this, the bubble is observed to detach laterally from the rim of the orifice rather than forming a stable axisymmetric cap~\cite{sasetty23stability}.

The study of spreading bubble departure goes back to Fritz~\cite{fritz35berechnung}, who numerically determined the maximum bubble volume as a function of the contact angle $\phi_c$, obtaining the expression $V=27.5\lambda^3\phi_c^3$. Phan et al.~\cite{phan09surface} present an empirical model for the departure size of bubbles in nucleate pool boiling. Recent models for the detachment size account for contact angle hysteresis~\cite{degennes85wetting} and use the advancing and receding~\cite{allred21role,demirkir24life} contact angles or dynamic contact angles~\cite{mukherjee07numerical} as inputs.

We unify past research by treating pinned and spreading behaviour within a single framework. We address two fundamental questions: 1.~\emph{How large does a bubble grow before it detaches?} And 2.~\emph{How does it detach?} We answer these questions by considering a simplified system consisting of a circular cavity of radius $r_c$ containing an already-nucleated bubble, surrounded by a smooth horizontal surface with a non-hysteretic contact angle $\phi_c$; the fluid is quiescent, so fluid forces are purely hydrostatic. This system is physically equivalent to a pendant drop. Throughout this Letter we refer to the system as a bubble, but our results apply equally to pendant drops. We determine the detachment size for pinned and spreading bubbles across the full range of cavity sizes, capillary lengths ($0\le r_c/\lambda\le\infty$), and contact angles ($0\le\phi_c\le\pi$).

To calculate the bubble shape, we use the method developed by~\citet{fritz35berechnung}; consider a bubble attached to a solid horizontal surface, as depicted in figure~\ref{figProfile}; $z$ denotes the height above the substrate. The interface of the bubble satisfies the Young--Laplace equation~\cite{degennes04capillarity}:
\begin{equation}\label{eqLaplace}
\sigma \left( \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} \right) = p_\mathrm{in} - p_\mathrm{out},
\end{equation}
where $R_1$ and $R_2$ are the principal radii of curvature at a point on the interface, and $p_\mathrm{in}$, $p_\mathrm{out}$ are the pressures inside and outside the bubble, respectively.

Assuming both fluid phases are in hydrostatic equilibrium, the pressures at height $z$ are given in terms of reference pressures $p_\mathrm{0in}$ and $p_\mathrm{0out}$ at the substrate ($z = 0$):
\begin{align}
p_\mathrm{in}(z)  &= p_{0\mathrm{in}}  - \rho_\mathrm{in} g z, \\
p_\mathrm{out}(z) &= p_{0\mathrm{out}} - \rho_\mathrm{out} g z.
\end{align}
Substituting into equation~\ref{eqLaplace} and dividing through by the surface tension gives:
\begin{equation}\label{eqPressure}
  \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} = \frac{p_\mathrm{0in} - p_\mathrm{0out}}{\sigma} + \frac{z}{\lambda^2}.
\end{equation}
To eliminate the pressure difference $p_\mathrm{0in} - p_\mathrm{0out}$, we evaluate equation~\ref{eqPressure} at the apex of the bubble, where $z = h$ and the interface is spherical, so $R_1 = R_2 = R_\mathrm{h}$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{2}{R_\mathrm{h}} = \frac{p_\mathrm{0in} - p_\mathrm{0out}}{\sigma} + \frac{h}{\lambda^2}.
\end{equation}
Subtracting this from equation~\ref{eqPressure} gives:
\begin{equation}\label{eqPressCurv}
\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} = \frac{2}{R_\mathrm{h}} + \frac{z - h}{\lambda^2}.
\end{equation}
Assuming the bubble is axisymmetric about the vertical axis, we describe the interface shape in terms of arc length $s$ from the apex, distance from the axis $r$, and the angle $\phi$ between the interface tangent and the horizontal. The two principal curvatures are:
\begin{equation}
R_1 = \frac{ds}{d\phi}, \qquad R_2 = \frac{r}{\sin\phi}.
\end{equation}
Substituting into equation~\ref{eqPressCurv} and multiplying by the capillary length yields the governing equation for the bubble shape:
\begin{equation}\label{eqShape}
\lambda\frac{d\phi}{ds} + \frac{\lambda\sin\phi}{r} = \frac{2\lambda}{R_\mathrm{h}} + \frac{z - h}{\lambda}.
\end{equation}
This equation governs the curvature of an axisymmetric interface under gravity. Note that each length scale appears as a ratio of the capillary length, and hence the bubble shapes are self-similar; the results scale universally with $\lambda$. We solve it using a shooting method, starting from the apex at $r=0$, $z=h$, $\phi=\pi$, with an initial guess for $R_h$. We integrate along the arc $s$ using the Adams--Bashforth method~\cite{bashforth83attempt}, which was originally developed to solve this very equation. The code and plotted data are freely available at~\cite{cannon26bubble}.

For pinned bubbles (mode~A~\cite{chesters78modes}), integration is stopped when $r=r_c$. Figure~\ref{figProfile}a shows profiles for a sequence of cavity radii. We see that each bubble begins with a flat interface across the cavity, and the contact angle decreases non-monotonically from $\phi_0=\pi$ as the bubbles inflate. For spreading bubbles (mode~B~\cite{chesters78modes}), integration is stopped when $\phi=\phi_c$. Figure~\ref{figProfile}b shows profiles for a sequence of contact angles. Here we see the contact radii increase non-monotonically from $r_0=0$ as the bubbles inflate.

To understand the evolution of a growing pinned bubble, we plot its volume $V$ and height $h$ in figure~\ref{figHeight}a, where the volume is computed as the integral of stacked cylinders,
\begin{equation}
V=\int_0^h\pi r^2\,dz.
\end{equation}
When a bubble nucleates inside the cavity, it has zero volume and zero height and begins at the bottom-left of figure~\ref{figHeight}a. As the bubble grows, its height $h$ increases and it remains pinned on the $r_c$ curve until one of three conditions is met:
\begin{enumerate}
\item If the foot angle $\phi_0$ falls below the contact angle $\phi_c$ of the surrounding surface, the contact line unpins and spreads across the surface~\cite{chesters78modes}. The bubble then follows a $\phi_c$ curve in figure~\ref{figHeight}b.
\item If the foot angle $\phi_0$ reaches $\pi$, the interface becomes unstable to non-axisymmetric perturbations~\cite{michael76equilibrium}, the interface peak shifts to one side of the cavity, and the bubble detaches laterally. This occurs for $r_0>3.219\,\lambda$, at the right of figure~\ref{figHeight}a.
\item If the bubble volume reaches the maximum of the $r_c$ curve, the height can increase without a further increase in volume and the bubble detaches axisymmetrically. This corresponds to the terminal point of each $r_c$ curve in figure~\ref{figHeight}a.
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{figHeight}b shows the possible volumes and heights of spreading bubbles for contact angles across the full range $0\le\phi_c\le\pi$. As with pinned bubbles, each curve has a maximum volume at which the bubble height can instantaneously increase; this is the detachment volume of the spreading bubble. The greater the contact angle $\phi_c$, the larger the detachment volume $V$. We note that the tallest possible bubble has $h=2.642\,\lambda$, which is considerably less than the analytical limit of $h=3.42\,\lambda$ established for two-dimensional pendant drops~\cite{sumesh10possible}.

Figure~\ref{figPinVol}(a) shows the detachment volume $V_p$ of pinned bubbles. The detachment volume increases with cavity radius, following a trend close to the Tate prediction~\cite{tate64magnitude}; deviations arise due to the non-spherical interface shape under buoyancy. These deviations are confirmed by experimental measurements of bubbles emitted from tubes in water~\cite{lesage13experimental}, bubbles in carbonated water from millimetric cavities~\cite{mori01bubble}, and pendant drops~\cite{gunde01measurement}. Figure~\ref{figPinVol}b shows the foot angle $\phi_0$ at departure. Contrary to Chesters' assumption~\cite{chesters77analytical}, the foot angle at detachment exceeds $\pi/2$; for cavities with $r_c>3.219\,\lambda$, $\phi_0$ reaches $\pi$ and the interface becomes unstable to non-axisymmetric perturbations~\cite{michael76equilibrium}, causing the bubble to slide to one side and detach laterally. No stable solution exists for $r_0>3.832\,\lambda$~\cite{plateau73statique}; above this radius, equation~\ref{eqShape} has no solution with $\phi_0<\pi$ and lateral detachment is immediate, consistent with the dripping behaviour reported in sessile drop experiments~\cite{sasetty23stability}. Figure~\ref{figPinVol}b also shows the minimum foot angle reached during bubble evolution. If the surface contact angle $\phi_c$ exceeds this minimum, the bubble will spread across the surface.

Figure~\ref{figSpreVol}a shows the detachment volume $V_s$ of spreading bubbles. For small contact angles the Fritz prediction is accurate. As $\phi_c\to\pi$, however, the detachment volume saturates below Fritz's extrapolation at a maximum of $V=18.96\,\lambda^3$. Our results are confirmed by measurements of water electrolysis~\cite{demirkir24life}, boiling~\cite{allred21role}, and air bubbles over superhydrophobic surfaces~\cite{huang26effects}. Figure~\ref{figSpreVol}b shows the radius of the bubble foot at departure and the maximum radius of the foot during bubble evolution. In all cases, the maximum foot radius exceeds the departure radius, indicating that the contact line initially spreads and then partially recedes prior to detachment. In some instances, the foot radius may again equal the cavity radius $r_c$, re-pinning the bubble at the cavity, in which case the departure volume corresponds to $V_p$.

We now return to the two questions posed at the outset. \textit{Answer to Q1:} The detachment volume depends on whether the bubble is pinned or spreading. For pinned bubbles, the detachment volume rises nearly linearly with cavity radius, closely following the Tate prediction $V = 2\pi\lambda^2 r_c$, with quantitative corrections arising from the non-spherical interface shape under buoyancy (figure~\ref{figPinVol}). For spreading bubbles, the detachment volume increases monotonically with contact angle and saturates at $V_s = 18.96\,\lambda^3$ as $\phi_c \to \pi$, below the Fritz extrapolation (figure~\ref{figSpreVol}). Both results are confirmed by independent experimental datasets spanning electrolytic, boiling, and carbonated bubbles, as well as pendant drops. \textit{Answer to Q2:} The detachment mode is determined by which threshold is reached first during bubble growth (figure~\ref{figTree}). A pinned bubble detaches axisymmetrically from the cavity centre when its volume reaches the maximum of the $r_c$ curve; it detaches laterally when the foot angle reaches $\phi_0 = \pi$, which occurs only for $r_c > 3.219\,\lambda$; and it transitions to spreading mode when the foot angle $\phi_0$ falls below the contact angle $\phi_c$ of the surrounding surface. A spreading bubble then detaches from the surface when its volume reaches $V_s$. No stable bubble exists for $r_c > 3.832\,\lambda$, above which lateral detachment is immediate.

In summary, we have computed the maximum stable volume of axisymmetric bubbles on flat surfaces as a function of contact radius and contact angle, using a numerical solution of the Young--Laplace equation. The results unify the pinned and spreading detachment modes within a single framework, recover the classical Tate and Fritz limits, and identify the thresholds at which non-axisymmetric instabilities govern departure. All results scale universally with the capillary length $\lambda$ and apply equally to pendant drops. These findings provide quantitative guidance for designing electrodes with optimised wettability and cavity geometry to promote early bubble detachment and improve electrolysis efficiency. Our model can be extended to account for heterogeneous surfaces, which give rise to contact angle hysteresis.
