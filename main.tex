\begin{abstract}
We measure the detachment size of bubbles on flat surfaces. The results highlight how the maximum bubble volume depends on contact angles and capillary length, and generalize to pendant drops.
\end{abstract}

Hydolysis efficiency is limited by attached bubbles on the electrode, which hinder the movement of H+ ions and increase resistive losses. Hence, it is essential to optimise electrode properties in order to ensure that bubbles detatch from the electrode before they become large.

Pendant drops are physically equivalent to attached bubbles. We shall henceforth refer to the system as a bubble, but the results apply to both.

Pinned bubbles are referred to as mode A by Chesters~\cite{chesters78modes}, whereas spreading is mode B.

The Tate volume of pinned bubbles \cite{tate64magnitude} balances buoyancy and surface tension, assuming a spherical cap with $\phi_0=\pi/2$.

Demirkir~\cite{demirkir24life} developed a formula for the bubble detachment size which depends on the advancing and receding contact angles.

Allred et al.~\cite{allred21role} include spreading and receding contact angles in a model of detachment size.

Phan et al.~\cite{phan09surface} model departure size for bubbles with contact angles less than $90^\circ$.

The bubble becomes unstable to an asymmetrical perturbation when the contact radius exceeds $3.219\,\lambda$~\cite{michael76equilibrium}, and the bubble is observed to detach from the side of the orifice~\cite{}.

It is impossible to form stable drops under constant pressure if the radius of the tube exceeds $2.40483\,\lambda$~\cite{pitts74stability}, and under constant volume if the radius exceeds $3.832\,\lambda$~\cite{plateau73statique}. For orifices larger than this, For orifices larger than this, the liquid is observed to drip instead of forming a stable drop~\cite{sasetty23stability}.

Tocalculate the bubble shapre we use the method developred by~\citet{fritz35berechnung}. Consider a bubble as depicted in figure~\ref{figProfile}, it is attached to a solid horizontal surface, and $z$ is the height above the substrate. The interface of the bubble satisfies the Young--Laplace equation:
\begin{equation}\label{eqLaplace}
\sigma \left( \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} \right) = p_\mathrm{in} - p_\mathrm{out},
\end{equation}
where $\sigma$ is the surface tension, $R_1$ and $R_2$ are the principal radii of curvature at a point on the interface, and $p_\mathrm{in}$, $p_\mathrm{out}$ are the pressures inside and outside the bubble, respectively.

Assume both fluid phases are in hydrostatic equilibrium. Then the pressures at height $z$ are given in terms of reference pressures $q_\mathrm{in}$ and $q_\mathrm{out}$ at the substrate ($z = 0$):
\begin{equation}
p_\mathrm{in}(z) = q_\mathrm{in} - \rho_\mathrm{in} g z, \\
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
p_\mathrm{out}(z) = q_\mathrm{out} - \rho_\mathrm{out} g z,
\end{equation}
where $\rho_\mathrm{in}$ and $\rho_\mathrm{out}$ are the densities of the inner and outer fluids, and $g$ is the gravitational acceleration.

Substituting into equation~\ref{eqLaplace} gives:
\begin{equation}\label{eqHydro_laplace}
\sigma \left( \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} \right) = q_\mathrm{in} - q_\mathrm{out} + (\rho_\mathrm{out} - \rho_\mathrm{in}) g z.
\end{equation}
Let 
\begin{equation}
\lambda \equiv \sqrt\frac{\sigma}{(\rho_\mathrm{out} - \rho_\mathrm{in})g},
\end{equation}
be the capillary lengthscale~\cite{}, which sets the radius of curvature of the interface due to gravity. Divide through by the surface tension,
\begin{equation}\label{eqPressure}
  \frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} = \frac{q_\mathrm{in} - q_\mathrm{out}}{\sigma} + \frac{z}{\lambda^2}.
\end{equation}
To eliminate the pressure difference $q_\mathrm{in} - q_\mathrm{out}$, evaluate equation~\ref{eqPressure} at the apex of the bubble, where $z = h$ and the interface is spherical, so $R_1 = R_2 = R_\mathrm{h}$:
\begin{equation}
\frac{2}{R_\mathrm{h}} = \frac{q_\mathrm{in} - q_\mathrm{out}}{\sigma} + \frac{h}{\lambda^2}.
\end{equation}
Subtracting this from equation~\ref{eqPressure} gives:
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{R_1} + \frac{1}{R_2} = \frac{2}{R_\mathrm{h}} + \frac{z - h}{\lambda^2}.
\end{equation}
Multiply by the capillary length:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\lambda}{R_1} + \frac{\lambda}{R_2} = \frac{2\lambda}{R_\mathrm{h}} + \frac{z - h}{\lambda}.
\end{equation}
Each lentgthscale in this equation can be expressed as a ratio of the capillary length, and hence the bubble shapes are self-similar, the results scale universally with the capillary length $\lambda$. This can be understood rouglhy as the vertical scale over which the interface changes curvature due to the effect of buouyancy.

Assuming the bubble is axisymmetric about the vertical axis $x=a$, we describe the interface shape in terms of arc length $s$ from the apex, distance from the axies $x-a$, and the angle $\phi$ between the interface tangent and the horizontal. The two principal curvatures are:
\begin{equation}
R_1 = \frac{ds}{d\phi}, \\
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
R_2 = \frac{r}{\sin\phi}.
\end{equation}
Substituting into the pressure--curvature relation yields the governing equation for the bubble shape:
\begin{equation}\label{eqShape}
\lambda\frac{d\phi}{ds} + \frac{\lambda\sin\phi}{r} = \frac{2\lambda}{R_\mathrm{h}} + \frac{z - h}{\lambda}.
\end{equation}
This equation governs the curvature of an axisymmetric interface under gravity, balancing surface tension and hydrostatic pressure. We solve the equation using a shooting method, starting from the apex of the bubble at $x=a, z=h,$ with $\phi=\pi/2$ and an initial guess for the radius of curvature $R_h$. We integrate along the path $s$ using the Adams Bashforth step method~\cite{bashforth83attempt}.

To understand the evolution of a growing bubble which is pinned pinned to a cavity with radius $r_0$, we plot its volume $V$ and height $h$ in figure~\ref{figHeight}a, where the volume is the integral of stacked cylinders 
\begin{equation}
V=\int_0^h\pi r^2dz
\end{equation}
We measure the bubble volume above the contact line, however, some gas may remain attached to the surface after bubble breakage, so the true detached volume may be less. We assume that the bubble nucleates in a cavity of radius $r_0$ with zero volume and zero height, at the bottom left of figure~\ref{figHeight}. The height $h$ increases as the bubble grows. The bubble remains pinned, and lies on the $r_0$ curve until one of three conditions is met;
\begin{enumerate}
\item If the contact angle $\phi_0$ becomes less than the receeding contact angle on the surface surrounding the cavity, the contact line will become unpinned and spread across the surface, as described in~\cite{chesters78modes}. The bubble height and volume now follows a curve in figure~\ref{figHeight}b.
\item If the contact angle $\phi_0$ becomes equal to $\pi$, the interface becomes unstable to non-axissymmetric perturbations~\cite{michael76equilibrium}, the peak slides to one side of the cavity, and the bubble detaches from the side of the cavity. This occurs for $r_0>3.219\lambda$. **and the limiting case can be seen in figure~\ref{figHeight} a.**
\item If the volume of the bubble reaches the maximum possible volume for the $r_0$ curve, its height can increase without an increase in volume, and it detaches. This replresented by the end of each $r_0$ curve in figure~\ref{figHeight}a. 
\end{enumerate}

We see that, as with the case of pinned bubbles, each $\phi_0$ curve has a maximum volume at which the bubble height can instantaneously increase, this is the detatchment volume of the spreading bubble. Figure~\ref{figPinVol} shows the detachment volume $V$ of pinned bubbles with cavity radius $r_0$. We see that the detachment volume initially increases with cavity radius, and it follows a trend close to that of the Tate volume~\cite{tate64magnitude}. However, there are deviation due to the nonspherical shape of the interface under buoyancy effects. The deviations from the Tate volume are supported by experimental measurements of bubbles emitted from tubes in water~\cite{lesage13experimental}, and bubbles in carbonated water emitted from millimetric cavities~\cite{mori01bubble}, and pendant drops~\cite{gunde01measurement}. For cavities with $r_0>3.219$, the contact angle reaches $\phi_0=\pi$ and the interface becomes unstable to non-axissymmetric perturbations~\cite{michael76equilibrium}. The bubble slides to one side of the cavity, and detaches from the side of the cavity. This occurs for $r_0>3.219\lambda$. The maximum possible cavity which can support a bubble has radius $r_0=3.832\lambda$~\cite{plateau73statique}, above this radius the bubble will immediately detatch from the side of the cavity.

Figure~\ref{figSpreVol} shows the detatchment volume $V_s$ of spreading bubbles. We see that for small contact angles the Fritz detachment size is accurrate. However, when the contact angle approaches $\phi_c=\pi$, the detatchment size becomes smaller than Fritz' fit result, giving a maximum detachment volume of $V=18.96\lambda^3$. Our results are supported by measurements of water electrolysis~\cite{demirkir24life}, boiling~\cite{allred21role}, and air bubbles over superhydrophobic surfaces~\cite{huang26effects}.
